# Reputation Momentum

Reputation is not only a moral ledger. It is also a status market.

A public figure can survive a serious accusation longer than expected if they still feel culturally alive: people are still watching, buying, sharing, voting, laughing, or showing up. Momentum becomes evidence of public consensus. If everyone else still seems interested, the individual observer hesitates to conclude that the person is finished.

> "Momentum is cancellation's condom." — Chris Williamson

## Core Idea

Chris Williamson's frame is that cancellation has two different components:

- **Real cancellation** says: this person did something bad.
- **Cringe cancellation** says: this person became uncool.

The dangerous moment is when the two converge. Badness creates moral permission to condemn; cringe creates social permission to abandon. Until someone loses aura, a surprising amount of public disapproval can remain inert.

This explains why optics matter so much online. People are not only asking, "What happened?" They are asking, "What does everyone else think this means?" Public opinion behaves less like a court and more like a crowd waiting to see which way the conga line is moving.

## What We Can Learn

- Do not confuse being morally right with winning the status game; crowds process both at once.
- Momentum can delay consequences, but it can also hide weakness until the hype slows down.
- Reputational risk increases when people stop needing you to be cool, useful, or entertaining.
- In a crowd, perception compounds: people update not only on facts, but on everyone else's reaction to the facts.
