# You'll Never Feel Enough Until You Understand This - Alain de Botton

Source: [Nuvola — YouTube](https://youtu.be/Dv9v1Br83Vs)  
Speaker: Alain de Botton  
Duration: 15:10

## Practical takeaway:

The life that makes you feel “enough” is not the life with the most visible achievement. It is the life that honestly fits your own capacities, needs, wounds, and temperament. Envy, anxiety, ambition, and the desire for fame are not moral failures; they are diagnostic signals. Read them instead of obeying them blindly.

## Core ideas

- Not knowing what you want is normal. Desire often appears as a faint “beep” when you encounter a possible future self.
- Envy can be useful: it points toward something you might be capable of or secretly want.
- Happiness is not achieving everything. It is achieving what you feel you are capable of.
- One-size-fits-all success definitions are broken because people need different mixtures of money, time, nature, friendship, stimulation, and recognition.
- Anxiety can be “a worry that does not know itself”; depression can be “a sadness we have no space for.” Fighting the feeling often worsens it.
- A mature life requires tolerating your own averageness in many domains. The insistence on specialness can become malignant.
- The desire for fame often compensates for invisibility or a lack of love.
- Achievement can be powered by unhappiness. The problem is not ambition itself, but pretending achievement will resolve the wound underneath it.

You know, the trick of being happy is not to achieve anything and everything. It is to achieve what you feel you are capable of. I think that is what leads ultimately to happiness.

All success is based on unhappiness. I mean, what powers you? If you were fully at ease, you would not get out of bed.

**0:29** First of all, it is really normal to be puzzled by the question. I think sometimes people think, “I do not know what I really want to do; therefore there is not anything that I really want to do.” I think that is the wrong way around.

Not knowing is very, very normal. There are very few people who say, “I want to be a brain surgeon, and that is it, and I am just going to go for it, and I knew when I was 12, and now I am 93, and it was absolutely right for me.” Very few of us are like that.

Most of us have to — I sometimes think of it like passing a metal detector over the ground. Every now and then there is a sort of beep, at certain moments, when you are coming across a version, a future version, of yourself.

**1:03** I sometimes think a really good guide to what you might be doing, and who you should be, is envy.

Every time that you feel envious of someone or of a situation — someone tells you what they are doing and you feel you kind of hate them — if you use that hatred, it is often a sign that there is something that they are doing that you are capable of. That is why you have been slightly triggered by it: because you are recognizing a kind of future potential.

So using envy is, I think, a really useful step. Then slowly assemble the jigsaw puzzle.

Do various exercises: imagine 12 different scenarios of your future. Grade them on a scale of 1 to 10. In 10 years’ time I am a… how does that feel? In 10 years’ time it is a Tuesday morning and I am off to do… how does that feel?

Sometimes our minds are a little bit obtuse. You would think they would work that bit out, but no. We sometimes have to go through an almost childishly simple thought exercise to project ourselves forward and see how we might feel.

**2:04** I quite like that phrase “becoming the best version of yourself,” which we hear a lot, but what does it really mean?

I think we are always aware that there are days, periods of our lives, which honor our potential more than others. The trick of being happy is not to achieve anything and everything. It is to achieve what you feel you are capable of. I think that is what leads ultimately to happiness.

All of us are aware of a sort of latent potential in us. Especially at the beginning — when you are finishing school, maybe going on to higher education — you feel that potential in a very diffuse form. You sense there is something in you, but exactly what it is can take such a long time to work out.

A life where you have really exploited your own potential performance is one where you have located that sort of best self and put it to work. I think that is going to be happiness for you.

**3:00** It is very individual. That is why all definitions of success that aim for a one-size-fits-all are always going to be very wonky.

We know certain things, like the money-time equation, are going to fit different people differently. But there are also other things: how much nature do you need around you? How many friends do you need around you? How much intellectual stimulation do you need?

There are such individual answers, and finding those inner keys to your musical score is going to be absolutely essential.

**3:36** Sometimes we are a bit too addicted to a positive image of ourselves, and we take fright too easily at things that would suggest we are more complicated, maybe not so normal. We infer so quickly that we cut ourselves off from some of our richer, more complicated possibilities.

Think about this in relation to anger. Many of us get upset very often by people close to us, by little things that they do. That hurt and anger can accumulate. If we do not have a safe way to discuss it, to discharge it, what can happen is that we go numb. We may get weighed down by a diffuse but unanalyzable sense of despair, or we flinch when they come close to us. We do not want them near us.

The real reason is that there is something we need to get off our chest, but because we are too rigidly keen on thinking of ourselves as good people — very loving, kind, and upbeat — we do not allow ourselves to go to some of these darker places. Then the problems start to accumulate.

**4:40** Take an emotion like anxiety. I think anxiety is often a worry that does not know itself.

Why does it not know itself? Often because the anxiety is contrary to something we want to believe to be true about ourselves. We do not want to feel alarmed about something because it has no place in our image of ourselves or of the world.

Similarly, depression is often a sadness that we have no space for within our conception of what is normal for us.

Imagine someone left us five years ago. “Well, why? We are a normal person. Why would we still be upset about that? That is not possible.” Actually, the mind is a strange and complex organ. Maybe we are.

Allowing room for some of these more unusual ways in which our minds work can actually leave us feeling lighter in ourselves.

**5:27** Are you saying that for someone with anxiety, part of the anxiety is caused by fighting the feeling?

One hundred percent. We are, as the old expression has it, our own worst enemies. We are fighting civil wars. Another useful expression is being at peace with oneself.

We may be at war with others, with life, with situations, but so long as we are not at war with ourselves, that is already a big burden off us. Often we are fighting these wars.

Take somebody who feels they need to adhere to a certain religious code, but at another level there is something in that code that is brushing up against something pretty substantial in their nature. There will be a constant, unhelpful war.

A lot of feelings that cause us most trouble are those where there is a refusal to accept that we might be a certain thing, or a certain way.

**6:26** I think we have a really conflicted vision of the good life in our societies, particularly Anglo-American societies.

On the one hand, we are democracies, so we believe in the legitimacy of every single voice. Everyone needs to be counted. These are also societies with a predominantly Christian background, and Christianity very much emphasizes the dignity of every life and every human.

But at the same time, these are societies that are pretty aggressively hierarchical, where there is a sense that the winner should take, if not all, a substantial part, and that mediocrity is associated with being like everyone else.

If you said to a young person nowadays in the United States or United Kingdom, “I am aiming for a life where I am, in many ways, just like everybody else,” that would be seen as dispiriting and depressing.

We think very well of drama and melodrama. Many great things are achievable perhaps only with a certain level of heightened activity and stress. But the ability to tolerate one’s own averageness in many fields is the beginning of a certain sort of wisdom, and the insistence always on specialness is a kind of malignant situation.

**7:47** At The School of Life, we like the phrase that if you have done well as a parent, your child should have no interest in being famous.

Nowadays, a lot of people want to be famous. It is a very common aspiration. That is a sign that something is failing us in our understanding of an ordinary existence.

**8:13** I believe that an exaggerated desire for fame, for distinction, is often trying to compensate for a feeling of invisibility.

The more invisible someone feels, the more they feel that they must be up on a stage of some sort, in a spotlight.

Scratch below the desire for fame. What is it for? What is it really about? Here I am talking about desire for fame rather than desire for distinction on the basis of something you are doing.

It is really the desire that other people admire you: strangers, people who do not know you, thinking well of you. It often hides a sad story of some form of invisibility or injury.

The more we create a society in which you do not really exist until you have achieved distinction, it becomes a kind of arms race. Then you have to achieve distinction because there is no other way of getting that distinction.

Of course, what is the first question everybody gets asked in the modern world? “What do you do for a living?”

We work for money, of course, but we also work in order to have an answer to that question, which will deliver us that other thing we so want: a sense of being seen and respected. It is such a powerful human driver.

**9:30** We speak of people feeling solid inside or not solid. What does that really mean?

I think a child who has had an extremely intense period of having felt at the center of the world grows up into someone who can tolerate not being at the center of the world.

Sometimes, in our story of parenting, we exaggerate the dangers of giving children the limelight when they are very little — I am talking one year old. There is a feeling of, “Oh no, that kid is getting spoiled.” They have been on the earth for all of 12 months and they are spoiled. I do not see such a danger there.

I see a much greater danger in under-privileging, under-spoiling, if you like, someone in the very early years, which then sets up a dynamic that they have to be aggressively on stage thereafter.

**10:20** Look, all success is based on unhappiness. What powers you if you were fully at ease? You would not get out of bed.

The drive to success is something itching you. It is really good if your parents die at a key age, your parents go bankrupt at a key age. I did not have either of those, but parents definitely seem like… parents who do not like you or seem to have… You have that, right? Your dad was keen on you.

**10:46** You might be the perfect guest for this subject. I think you have done TED Talks on success, have you not? It is obviously something you have thought about an enormous amount. But let us unpack what you just said. So success is based on unhappiness?

Yes. The human animal is very lazy. What gets us thinking is a problem.

If you want to look at it psychotherapeutically, psychotherapists say that the beginning of thought is the moment when the mother’s breast does not come to give milk to the baby. Until then the baby thinks, “The world is pretty good. Whenever I think of something, there it appears.” Suddenly there is no breast, there is no milk, and the baby thinks, “What is going wrong?” The baby goes crazy and starts to think.

The beginning of thinking is frustration.

Whenever there is a problem, you can do two things: lie down and die, or try to find a way around it. The quality of your thought tends to be commensurate with the scale of the obstacle.

**11:51** You start to think about love. I started thinking about love; relationships started fascinating me in my teenage years.

I wrote my first book about relationships, *Essays in Love*, when I was 22. Sometimes people say, “Oh God, you must know a lot about that. This must be an area you are flourishing in, in order to write.” Obviously not. It is always the opposite.

You go back and think, “That was quite presumptuous, to be a 22-year-old writing about that.”

I just feel sorry for myself. I think, “What a problematic kid this guy was.”

All outsized achievements are powered by an outsized problem. You can always tell what the problem is by looking at what the thing is that they are achieving. Someone who makes a lot of money is obviously laboring under an incredible sense of deprivation. It is literally the opposite.

**12:38** Is Elon Musk powered by unhappiness?

Of course. Yes.

Is Trump powered by unhappiness?

Of course. A kind of restlessness.

This is the paradox: we associate happiness with achievement, but we know that is not quite true. The people who are really, really content and at ease with themselves — we have never heard of them. They die in unmarked graves. We have just never heard of those people.

What lives did they lead, roughly speaking? They were not anxious and trying to compete with their dad? Is that what it is?

They felt seen without being famous.

**13:15** The whole urge to be famous — the reason why we are in a podcast — we are compensating for something. No one ends up on a podcast by coincidence.

If I were totally happy when your team called me up and said, “Do you want to appear on a podcast with Richard?” I would have said, “No, I am completely happy.” Something was gnawing at me: a sense of invisibility, a feeling that I have been neglected, a lack of love. Broadly, it is a lack of love.

Fame is about a lack of love. Of course you are massively compensated.

This is the irony: people want to be treated nicely on the basis that they are famous, and they are a little bit. But then, of course, you become a target for other people’s envy, because other people have been neglected. Then you attract a huge amount of envy.

It is the very people who are most sensitive to attacks on them who become famous, which is why you get this weird relationship between the audience and the famous person. The famous person is so vulnerable to the audience — that is why they have become famous — and the audience is secretly so envious of the famous person.

Then there is this sadomasochistic impulse to whip the guy who is famous. It assuages one’s sense of being a random bit of biology in a lifeless universe. It is calming, a little bit. But there is a price.

**14:35** I have felt the price of it. Again, I have paid a bit of it, but definitely, right at the beginning of my career, I got brought down in a big scandal. There are various prices to pay for it, but the drive comes from not being loved at some level.

Yes. Or being brought up in an environment that felt very chaotic. You are trying to secure something that feels extremely…

The sign of being a good parent is that your child has no ambition to be famous. If your kids have no desire to follow you in any way into show business, you will have done really well.
