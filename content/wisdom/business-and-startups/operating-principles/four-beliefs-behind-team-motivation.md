# Four Beliefs Behind Team Motivation

Source: [Angela Duckworth and Nordic Business Forum — Instagram carousel](https://www.instagram.com/p/DehjlmHlJ_6/).

## Practical takeaway:

Before treating low effort as a character problem, check whether the person finds the work interesting, understands its importance, believes they can do it, and knows what to try next.

## The four thoughts

The carousel presents these four thoughts as characteristic of the high performers Duckworth has studied:

1. **“My work is interesting.”**
2. **“This is important.”**
3. **“I can do this.”**
4. **“I know what to try next.”**

Duckworth connects being able to affirm all four with bringing passion and perseverance to work. In the caption, she invites leaders to ask whether their teammates have all four thoughts, arguing that a missing one limits full effort.

## Applying this in a startup

The following is an application of the framework, not a transcript of the post.

### Interest: does the work engage them?

Ask which parts of the role they are curious about and which feel mechanical. Look for opportunities to give them more ownership of problems that engage their strengths. Not every task needs to be fascinating, but the role should contain something worth leaning into.

**One-on-one prompt:** What part of your work would you like to spend more time on?

### Importance: can they connect the task to a worthwhile outcome?

Explain who benefits, what changes for the customer, and why this is a priority now. A task can be clear without its purpose being clear. Do not assume that a founder's conviction automatically transfers to the team.

**One-on-one prompt:** What do you think this work changes, and for whom?

### Confidence: do they believe success is possible?

Separate a skill gap from a lack of support, an unrealistic scope, or an unclear standard. Offer useful feedback, examples, coaching, and smaller milestones rather than encouragement alone.

**One-on-one prompt:** What makes this feel achievable or out of reach?

### Next step: do they have a way forward?

A person can care deeply and believe in their ability while still being stuck. Help identify a concrete experiment, a person to consult, or a small action that will reduce uncertainty.

**One-on-one prompt:** What will you try next, and what would you learn from it?

## Use it as a diagnostic, not a verdict

The four questions offer a useful starting point for a conversation, not a complete explanation of performance. Check workload, competing priorities, resources, and constraints too. Do not use the framework to dismiss exhaustion or label someone unmotivated.

A practical leadership response is to identify the missing condition and change something concrete: task design, context, support, or the next step.
