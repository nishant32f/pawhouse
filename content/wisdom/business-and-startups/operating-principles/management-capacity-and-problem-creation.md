# Management Capacity and Problem Creation

> “The only constraint on our imagination is management capacity.” — Travis Kalanick

Ambition does not fail only because the idea is bad. It often fails because the organization creates problems faster than it can solve them.

Kalanick calls this the meta problem: the rate of problem solving must be greater than or equal to the rate of problem creation. Launching a city, entering China, building a robotics stack, adding a business unit, or pushing into a regulated market all create future problems. Some do not come ashore for months. The founder’s job is not to avoid problems. It is to predict their shape, create the right ones, and build enough capacity to absorb them.

## The operating equation

```text
problem-solving capacity ≥ problem-creation rate
```

If the equation is healthy, imagination can become real. The company can widen scope, enter harder markets, and attack larger systems.

If the equation breaks, the organization starts drowning. The right move is not more inspiration. It is to slow problem creation and increase solving capacity: leaders, judgment, process, recruiting, tooling, and decision clarity.

## Problems are the material

Kalanick treats problems like a mathematician treats questions. A math professor without interesting problems is a sad math professor. Likewise, a founder without hard problems is probably not working on something important enough.

The distinction is between *interesting problems* and *unbounded chaos*:

- Interesting problems create leverage when solved.
- Chaos creates motion without learning.
- Good founders choose difficulty deliberately.
- Bad operators mistake uncontrolled fires for ambition.

## Super pumped

“Super pumped” is infectious enthusiasm for the hard, weird, gnarly thing. The best problems are often the ones people call impossible but are actually possible if the team can take enough pain and build enough capacity.

This is useful founder fuel, but incomplete. Enthusiasm must be paired with capacity. Otherwise “we love hard things” becomes a pretty banner over a burning building.

## Pain, but not numbness

“Excellence is the capacity to take pain” is true in the narrow entrepreneurial sense: the competitor willing to endure more discomfort can often keep going longer.

But pain has two failure modes:

1. **Fragility:** avoiding hard things because they hurt.
2. **Numbness:** becoming so used to adversity that wrong things stop provoking urgency.

The better target is Kalanick’s later formulation: fierceness with calm on the inside. Still bothered by what is wrong. Still intense enough to act. No longer internally chaotic.

## Dense rules beat bureaucracy

Leadership is finding the line between order and chaos. Too much order creates bureaucracy and slow, demoralized teams. Too much chaos creates confusion and slow, demoralized teams.

The practical version: use the fewest number of rules while staying out of chaos.

Uber’s city-launch pricing call is a good example. One rule — no city launches until pricing is approved — forced teams to understand regulation, vehicle type, driver economics, demand, geography, and strategy. The rule was dense. It controlled the system without burying people in process.

## Wisdom

- Do not ask only, “What can we imagine?” Ask, “What can we manage?”
- Creating problems is strategy. Creating more than you can solve is self-harm.
- Management capacity is not bureaucracy; it is the amount of useful problem-solving the organization can sustain.
- One good rule should force understanding of the whole system.
- Pain is a signal and a price, not an identity.
- The founder’s job is to keep the company close enough to chaos to move fast, but not so close that people stop learning.
