# Criticism stings because we want to be accepted

It is more socially acceptable to be your own biggest critic than your own biggest cheerleader, so outside criticism is easier to believe than it should be.

People avoid the pain with these strategies, with mixed success:

- Call critics haters ("Don't take criticism from someone you wouldn't take advice from").
- Use it as fuel to prove people wrong, as David Goggins does.
- Treat every critic as a gift to learn from.
- Read it as a window into other minds and draw sweeping conclusions about the public.
- Use very precise language so nothing is misconstrued.
- Front-run it with caveats ("I'm just an idiot spitballing a broscience theory") or acknowledge both sides first.
- Never search your name or read comments.
- Dampen opinions until nobody can object.
- Deny it affects you and distract with breathwork, busyness, meditation, scrolling or BJJ.
- Take it to heart and doubt you can handle any exposure.

You can build explanations from childhood, anxious attachment or inferiority complexes. Chris suspects the plainer cause is wanting to be accepted and liked, which feels bad when threatened.

Common wisdom says only true criticisms sting. Chris disagrees. Untrue criticisms that others might believe make him more indignant. Risking your reputation over something shameful you did not do feels worse than risking it over something you did. The opinions of people who dislike you, misunderstand your aims and enjoy being mean carry no weight alone. They matter because others might believe the misjudgement.

Go gentle on yourself. If your confidence was hard won, protect it.
