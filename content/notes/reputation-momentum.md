# Reputation holds while momentum looks like approval

Reputation depends on social status as well as moral judgment. A public figure can outlast a serious accusation if they still feel culturally alive, with people watching, buying, sharing, voting, laughing or showing up. Momentum becomes evidence of public consensus. If everyone else is still interested, an individual hesitates to conclude the person is finished.

> "Momentum is cancellation's condom." — Chris Williamson

Williamson's model splits cancellation in two.

- **Real cancellation** says this person did something bad. It gives moral permission to condemn.
- **Cringe cancellation** says this person became uncool. It gives social permission to abandon.

Public consequences tend to accelerate only when the crowd makes both judgments at once, so the person is seen as bad and has lost their aura. Until then a lot of disapproval can sit unused. Momentum shields a public figure from reputational contagion until the hype slows, and scrutiny rises once it does.

People ask "What happened?" and also "What does everyone else think this means?" Public opinion often follows what the crowd does rather than a verdict.

- Being morally right is different from winning the status game, and crowds process both together.
- Momentum delays consequences but can also hide weakness until the hype slows.
- Reputational risk rises when people stop needing you to be cool, useful or entertaining.
- In a crowd, perception compounds, because people update on everyone else's reaction to the facts as well as on the facts.
