# Reaching a goal does not deliver the state you expected

Arrival fallacy is the belief that reaching a milestone will produce the inner state you are chasing, such as peace, confidence, security, status or permission to enjoy life. Arthur Brooks cites the gold medal, the bestseller and getting rich, and notes that Olympic gold medalists often become depressed after winning.

Before arrival the goal feels like a condition to meet ("Once I get the job." "Once I make enough money." "Once the person chooses me."). After arrival the mind adjusts, the exceptional becomes normal, and you start looking toward the next goal.

Goals are not the mistake, since satisfaction comes from progress toward something difficult. The mistake is expecting a goal to settle a psychological need. If it was meant to deliver identity, belonging, love or meaning, success disappoints even when real. You got what you wanted and still brought yourself along.

Brooks says nature wants us fooled. Desire keeps people striving and the nervous system keeps promising the next one will work. The lesson is hard to pass on, because telling someone the summit will not satisfy them reduces their motivation.

People look past a goal while attaining it, ready to set a new target, and never savour the small completions on the way (Joe Hudson and Brett Kistler, via Chris Williamson). Anticipation can beat arrival, so enjoy the pursuit with small celebrations, visible milestones and attention to how the effort feels.

Questions to ask:

- What will this goal improve materially, and what will it not fix emotionally?
- What part of life must be lived before arrival?
- What daily process would still be worthwhile if the outcome took longer?
- Which need am I pretending this milestone will solve?
