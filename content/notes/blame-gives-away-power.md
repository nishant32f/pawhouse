# Blaming something gives it power over you

Alex Hormozi does not call disadvantage imaginary. Some people start with heavier weights, such as abuse, poverty, deformity, race, gender, language, family chaos, illness or social disadvantage. That can make success harder and may be unfair and not their fault. What remains is what you do about it.

His first step out of poverty or mediocrity is two words, "My fault." It is not self-blame. It reclaims power, because whatever you blame is where you also cast power. Blame your parents, circumstances or zip code for failing and they control your life. You can be right about why your life is bad, or you can be rich, and not both.

Nobody owes you patience for your pain. A hard start explains difficulty but does not make others wait or lower the standard. Blame can be accurate and still useless. If blame is where your agency stops, whoever you blame keeps shaping your life after the original damage. The aim is to stop giving it power. It does not deny what happened.

Acting anyway turns the disadvantage into proof for others with similar backgrounds, and pain into capability rather than identity. Compassion here can say that it was hard, that it was not your fault and that the starting line was unfair. It can also say you still have to choose a direction. Being on someone's side long term means wanting them to win rather than only validating why they might lose.

When the mind reaches for blame, ask what power you give away by naming this as the reason you cannot move, what smallest action returns power to you, and how this could become proof for someone else later.

## Ownership runs both ways

Simon Sinek's second life lesson is "Sometimes you are the problem." Take all the credit for what you do right as long as you take responsibility for what you do wrong. This differs from absorbing other people's failures, covered in [[carrying-everything-becomes-self-abandonment|Carrying everyone else's load becomes self-abandonment]].
