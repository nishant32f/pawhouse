# Low effort often means a missing belief

An Angela Duckworth and Nordic Business Forum Instagram carousel lists four thoughts it says characterise the high performers she has studied.

1. "My work is interesting."
2. "This is important."
3. "I can do this."
4. "I know what to try next."

Duckworth ties affirming all four to bringing passion and perseverance to work and asks leaders whether teammates hold all four. A missing one limits full effort, so check which before calling low effort a character problem.

## Applying it in a startup

The checks and questions below are an application of the framework and are not in the post.

- **Interest.** Find which parts of the role the person finds engaging or mechanical and give more ownership of problems that suit their strengths. Not every task must be fascinating. *What part of your work would you like to spend more time on?*
- **Importance.** Explain who benefits, what changes for the customer and why it is a priority now. A clear task can have an unclear purpose, and a founder's conviction does not transfer on its own. *What do you think this work changes, and for whom?*
- **Confidence.** Separate a skill gap from missing support, unrealistic scope or an unclear standard. Offer feedback, examples, coaching and smaller milestones in addition to encouragement. *What makes this feel achievable or out of reach?*
- **Next step.** Someone can care and believe in their ability and still be stuck. Help identify an experiment, a person to consult or a small action that reduces uncertainty. *What will you try next, and what would you learn from it?*

## Limits

The questions start a conversation and do not explain performance alone. Check workload, competing priorities, resources and constraints too, and do not use the questions to dismiss exhaustion or label someone unmotivated. Change something concrete, such as task design, context, support or the next step.
