# An executive charts the course, sets the pace and holds the line

DHH splits an executive's essential work into three.

## Chart the course

Know where the company is going, what it is building and who it is for, or any activity looks like progress. No five-year plan is needed. Basecamp and HEY choose features every 6 to 8 weeks, which charts the course just in time at high resolution. DHH says calling yourself a "long-term thinker" invites self-indulgent reasoning, since it is easier to bluff from 30,000 ft than with imminent decisions in front of you. Someone has to say "This is what we're doing. Let's go."

## Set the pace

Work expands to fit the time allotted, and ambition shrinks as productivity declines. Be ambitious, bold and impatient. Demand more from the hours instead of more hours, and question the premise. Why this way, could it be done differently, are you preparing for remote contingencies, are you spending enough time where it counts? That takes knowing the work, since an executive far from it relies on second-hand information and outdated views. Boldness without insight is delusion. Credibility comes from pushing for a reach and making it. An executive who pushes for the impossible and delivers none of it loses credibility.

## Hold the line

Quality declines when nobody cares about it. Take it personally, so wrongness offends you and that energy halts work to redo it. If you let it slide, nobody else will. The line covers code that is a joy to read in three years, support staff with enough policy leeway to solve problems without giving away too much, and company-signed writing that does not make you cringe. Pay for quality. Seek bargains where good quality is cheap, but never be cheap. A culture of quality is built one product and process decision at a time.

## The standard

Do all three well and consistently, including when it is hard and does not look like it is working. Whether that is enough is mostly outside your hands, but this execution tends to pay off.
