# The brain prefers an imagined catastrophe to not knowing

One quoted claim is that "Humans never genuinely pursue happiness; they only pursue relief from uncertainty. Happiness emerges momentarily as a byproduct whenever uncertainty briefly disappears."

When the future cannot be predicted, the default is fear, worry and rumination. The imagined scenario crowds out what is actually happening. The nightmare people imagine is usually much worse than what could happen. It still helps in one way, because it briefly collapses a chaotic world into certainty. People dislike not knowing so much that they would rather imagine a catastrophe than face something unpredictable.

This is why the brain ruminates. It would rather fantasise a catastrophe than live with not knowing what comes next. Finding out how rumination serves you is the first step to stopping it (see [[overthinking-is-underfeeling|Overthinking is often underfeeling]]).

Typical cases are an uncertain medical diagnosis in the family when you cannot be there, and an argument with a partner while apart, when you cannot know how they feel overnight. Another is a letter sent to mend a friendship that has not been answered.

If you are unhappy, look first at where you are uncertain, and at where you want things to be different ([[wanting-things-different-holds-happiness-hostage|Wanting things to be different blocks happiness]]).
