# Good company makes you feel interesting

Jennie Jerome, Winston Churchill's mother, dined on consecutive nights with Prime Minister Disraeli and his rival Gladstone. Asked about them, she said "When I left the dining room after sitting next to Gladstone, I thought he was the cleverest man in England. But when I sat next to Disraeli, I left feeling that I was the cleverest woman."

Most people want to be charismatic, with compelling energy and exciting stories. The friends Chris Williamson loves to spend time with have what he calls reverse charisma. We usually judge how interesting people are and miss how interesting they make us feel. The questions are:

- How engaged is this person?
- How much of us can they tolerate?
- How much of our reality can they handle without us editing ourselves?
- How encouraging and reassuring are they?
- How much do they make us want to talk more?
- How comfortably can we sit in silence?

In short, how much of us do they get? If it is not much, we stay cautious.

A person feels interesting to the degree that they are familiar and at ease with extreme, sad, dark, agonizing and shameful things. If they are at home with their own strangeness, they help us feel at home with ours. We can safely talk around them about what they have felt safe exploring in themselves.

Designing your own charisma is a vague task, and Williamson says it has failed for people who treated it as a technique. Anyone can build reverse charisma by becoming curious, patient and encouraging. Some people feel interesting and some make us feel interesting. There is room for both, but on average our favourite people are the latter, a point he credits to Alain de Botton.
