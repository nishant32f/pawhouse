# Accurate warnings are dismissed when early or from a low-status source

The Cassandra effect is a valid warning being disbelieved, often with bad consequences. Apollo gave the Trojan princess Cassandra prophecy, then cursed her when she rejected him. Her prophecies would be accurate and never believed. She warned of the wooden horse and Troy's fall and was called mad.

Modern Cassandras are whistleblowers, researchers, analysts and employees. Examples are scientists warning of climate change before consensus, economists predicting crashes, security experts flagging vulnerabilities, employees raising ethics concerns and health professionals warning of pandemics.

## Why warnings get dismissed

- **Timing.** Right too early looks like wrong, and by the time the evidence is undeniable it is often too late.
- **Messenger credibility.** Without expected expertise, social capital or a clean record, a warner carries no weight.
- **Uncomfortable truths.** Warnings demand responsibility, a changed course or admitted mistakes, and denial is easier when consequences seem distant.
- **Optimism bias.** It helps individuals and hurts groups facing systemic risk.
- **Boy who cried wolf.** Earlier false alarms from the person or industry discredit a real one.

The more dire the warning, the less it is believed. Moderate concerns get a hearing and catastrophic ones get called hysteria, yet those need early attention most.

## Cases

- Brooksley Born warned of derivatives risk in the 1990s and was marginalised.
- Raghuram Rajan predicted the 2008 crisis and was mocked at Jackson Hole.
- Michael Burry saw the housing bubble and was called eccentric until proven right.
- Pre-9/11 al-Qaeda reports were filed away.
- Colonial Pipeline security concerns were ignored until the ransomware attack.
- Enron whistleblowers were ostracised before the collapse.
- Theranos skeptics were legally threatened before the fraud was exposed.

Challenger and Boeing 737 MAX are in [[the-messenger-paradox|Groups that punish messengers stop hearing bad news]].

## Cost to the warner

Stagnation or termination, reputation damage, isolation, blacklisting, moral injury, anger at willful ignorance, self-doubt ("Am I the crazy one?"), and vindication that feels hollow after the damage.

## If you are the one warning

- Find enough of the right allies and build coalitions of belief.
- Lead with questions, offer options instead of ultimatums, quantify the risk and propose solutions.
- Save credibility for the most critical threats.
- If internal channels fail, consider regulators, media or industry groups, knowing the personal cost.
- Warn clearly with evidence. Belief is not your responsibility. Documentation and safe delivery are in [[the-messenger-paradox|Groups that punish messengers stop hearing bad news]].

## If you are the one hearing it

- Separate message from messenger. A difficult person can be right.
- Test for false consensus. Have you verified the warning is wrong, or is everyone dismissing it? See [[we-misjudge-what-others-believe|We misjudge what others believe]].
- Ask what the evidence would look like if the warning were true, then look for it.
- Act on probabilities. A reasonable chance of serious harm justifies reasonable mitigation.
- Checking a false alarm almost always costs less than ignoring a real threat. When a warning points to something specific, such as the wooden horse, inspect it.

Resilient organisations institutionalise dissent (red teams, devil's advocates, whistleblower protections), reward early warning even when threats do not materialise, and separate prediction accuracy from social consequences.
