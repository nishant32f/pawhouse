# A question that punishes honest answers cannot be answered honestly

"What makes a woman attractive?" is a Milgram question. The social penalty for an unflattering answer is much higher than the reward for telling the truth, so the answers cannot be trusted, even from friends. The name comes from the Milgram experiment, where participants gave electric shocks for wrong answers.

Other examples back the respondent into a corner:

- "When did you stop beating your wife?"
- "Does your dad know you're gay?"
- "Why are white people/men/heterosexuals so ignorant to racism/misogyny/homophobia?"

Any conventional answer confirms the assumption in the question. It resembles a Kafka trap, where denying being X is taken as evidence of being X, since someone who is X would deny it. Defending yourself counts as proof of guilt, and the innocent are lumped with the guilty who are trying to escape punishment.

The way out is to call out the question. In practice this is mostly a rhetorical gimmick and it is not seen often.

The most common kind sounds like "Do you love Big Brother?" Any unacceptable answer, true or false, gets punished, and the punishment exceeds the reward for a true answer. Some Milgram questions are genuine, but often they only pretend to ask about the words. "Do you love Big Brother?" asks "Do you submit to my power?" or, more generally, "Will you agree with me, or suffer the consequences?"

> "When punishment for what people say becomes widespread, people stop saying what they really think and instead say whatever is needed to thrive in the social environment. Thus, limits on speech become limits on sincerity" ~ h/t J. Sanilac & Gurwinder Bhogal
