# Envy points at capabilities you may have

Not knowing what you want is normal. People wrongly infer that because they do not know what they want to do, nothing is there to find. Few people knew at 12 that they would be a brain surgeon and were right at 93. De Botton compares the search to passing a metal detector over the ground. Now and then it beeps, and you have come across a future version of yourself.

Envy is one of those beeps. When someone describes what they do and you almost hate them, the reaction often means you recognise a capability in yourself. Use it as information, then assemble the pieces.

## An exercise

Imagine about 12 scenarios for your future and grade each from 1 to 10. Try "In 10 years I am a..." and "In 10 years it is a Tuesday morning and I am off to do...". The mind is sometimes obtuse, and a very simple projection exercise shows how you would feel.

## Success is individual

One-size-fits-all definitions of success do not work. People need different amounts of money and time, nature, friends and intellectual stimulation. De Botton calls finding these individual answers the inner keys to your musical score. Everyone senses a latent potential, especially when finishing school, and it takes a long time to work out what it is.

## Check the whole life

Envy of a visible success skips its private cost. The person you admire may have paid with anxiety, family wounds, obsession, loneliness or an inability to feel satisfied. The price of being who you admire may be one you would not pay. This matters because comparison is uneven. You see your own confusion from the inside and other people's highlights from the outside.
