# Love that must be earned is not love

Arthur Brooks says many high achievers learned early that love is earned. Parents praised grades, trophies and performances, thinking they were building success. The child learned that "I am loved when I perform."

In adulthood the pattern takes different forms.
- Men try to earn love through money or status.
- Women may try to earn love through youth or beauty.
- Creators seek the adoration of strangers.
- Leaders surround themselves with sycophants.
- Strivers become addicted to winning and specialness.

Brooks's corrective:

> "anybody who makes you earn their love doesn't love you"

Real love is a gift, freely given, a grace. Fame proves attention and approval proves reaction, and neither is love.

Specialness is a false substitute. People give up ordinary happiness, such as family, ordinary work and ordinary friendship, for a fantasy of being special, and that path tends to end badly because specialness cannot supply unconditional love. Brooks tells of a wealthy finance figure who admitted he had thought getting rich would make his wife really love him. It did not, and the man seemed to see the belief only on hearing himself say it.

Chris Williamson names an online culture, "grind slop", that says to ignore feelings and work harder, treating optimisation and achievement as moral proof. He says he has been moving away from purely actionable content because constant optimisation flattens experience. Sometimes the point is to be alive during a conversation and not to extract maximum use from it.
