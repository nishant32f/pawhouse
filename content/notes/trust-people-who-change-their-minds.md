# Trust thinkers who change their minds and admit mistakes

Four questions gauge the honesty of the creators you follow.

| Question | Rational thinker | Ideological thinker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do they change their mind? | Regular opinion updates | Never or rarely changes stance |
| How do they bond a group? | Shared positive values | Attacking out-groups |
| Do they admit mistakes? | Genuinely and vulnerably | Performatively or never |
| Do they engage alternatives? | Look for truth in other views | Mock dissenters or stay in an echo chamber |

## 1. When did you last hear them change their mind?

If no evidence could change someone's opinion, the belief is ideological. When ego and stance fuse, objectivity declines, and Williamson respects least those who treat changing an opinion as destroying their sense of self. Someone who never alters their opinions is either an unserious thinker or a deity.

## 2. Do they bond their group by naming out-groups?

Charlatans commonly do this. An enemy quickly produces tribal affiliation, but a group bound by shared dislike is fragile and ends in a purity spiral that shames heretics. Check whether you love the work of the people you admire or only hate the people who hate them.

## 3. Do they admit mistakes without performing?

Openness is a costly signal that the person values accuracy over seeming perfect. Performative "mistake porn" is a way to deflect accusations of hubris.

## 4. Do they want other views for reasons besides mocking them?

If divergent voices appear only to look silly, rally out-group hatred or get clicks, the work is an echo chamber and not concerned with truth. Other views aid learning and show you can talk to people you disagree with without an argument.

Williamson says the list is incomplete and that he must apply it to himself as consumer and creator.
