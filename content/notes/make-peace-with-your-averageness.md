# Happiness is achieving what you are capable of

De Botton says the trick of being happy is to achieve what you feel you are capable of, which stops short of anything and everything. A life that has located your best self and put it to work is the happy one. Some days and periods honor your potential more than others, which is what "becoming the best version of yourself" can usefully mean.

## Averageness

Anglo-American societies hold two views at once. As democracies with a Christian background they say every voice and life has dignity. They are also highly hierarchical, expect the winner to take a substantial part, and treat mediocrity as being like everyone else. A young person aiming for a life like everybody else would be seen as depressing.

Drama and melodrama get praise, and some great things may need heightened stress. Still, tolerating your own averageness in many fields is the beginning of wisdom, and insisting on being special is harmful.

## Accepting what you are

- People cling to a positive image of themselves and take fright at evidence they are more complicated. Hurt and anger toward people close to us pile up with no safe way to discuss it. We go numb, feel a diffuse despair or flinch at their approach, because we want to see ourselves as loving and upbeat.
- Anxiety is often a worry that does not know itself, because it contradicts how we want to see ourselves. Depression is often a sadness with no place in our idea of what is normal.
- Fighting the feeling makes it worse. De Botton says we fight civil wars, and being at peace with yourself removes a large burden even while you are at war with the world. Many troublesome feelings come from refusing to accept that we might be a certain way. An example is someone whose religious code presses against something real in their nature.
