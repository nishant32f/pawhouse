# Moving from operator to strategist means giving up busyness as identity

Early in a career, work rate is the only advantage. With no experience and talent capped by it, you answer every email, take every call, send invoices, write copy and hire contractors.

That stage ends when you get staff to delegate to or a promotion that needs strategic thinking. The job was to work hard and now it is not.

You built a routine that turns tedious tasks into completed work, and linked being busy with things you dislike to success. Emails sent and hours on calls are easy to count. What you want, and whether you are moving toward it, is hard to know. The sequence has four stages. Work rate is the only asset. You are promoted but still do everything. An identity crisis follows, because busy no longer equals success. Finally, success means vision and impact.

Ask whether your job is to press enter on emails or to move the mission forward. The pull is the dopamine of "I got stuff done today", which spares you from feeling useless or noticing you may not be using your potential.

Most people's goal is to not have to do anything they do not want to do. Much of self-worth comes from overcoming hard things, so reaching that goal removes a source of satisfaction. That is why grunt work and permanent busyness are hard to drop even when escaping them was the aim.

Success is also hard to enjoy because you are already looking at the next step and moving the goalposts while you reach the goal. Credit for these insights goes to Joe Hudson and Brett Kistler.
