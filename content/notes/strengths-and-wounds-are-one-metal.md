# Your strengths and your wounds come from the same source

Chris Williamson's line is that what you are praised for in public, you will pay for in private. A trait that makes someone effective at work, such as resilience, decisiveness or refusal to quit, can damage intimacy. Navy SEAL Andy Stumpf's inability to quit helped his career and also kept him in a toxic marriage much longer than he should have stayed.

Arthur Brooks puts it more broadly. Your strengths are your weaknesses and your weaknesses are your strengths. Shame, hypervigilance, uncertainty or fear can become attention to detail, drive, care and excellence. A strength that causes damage needs managing, and discarding it is not the answer. Brooks goes past acceptance. You need to love the weakness or wound as part of the full human package.

## The parental attribution error

People blame parents for their flaws and claim their strengths as their own. The same childhood that made you anxious also made you resilient. If you draw a line from childhood to flaws, draw the same line to strengths. If lack of freely given love produced drive, or unmet needs produced attunement to others, the story has no simple villain. The strength and the flaw have one source, and a harsh trait is often the downside of something useful.

Brooks calls this subversive because it challenges grievance culture. Identities built on grievance and victimhood produce misery, since they train people to spend energy complaining about the problem instead of working around or through it.
