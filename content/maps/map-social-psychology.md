# Social psychology and truth

How groups form beliefs, why truth-tellers get ignored, and how to judge sources.

## Groups and belief

- [[abilene-paradox|A group can choose what nobody wants]]. In the Abilene paradox a group agrees on a course of action that no member individually wants, because each assumes the others do.
- [[we-misjudge-what-others-believe|We misjudge what others believe]]. False consensus and pluralistic ignorance are opposite errors about what other people think, and silence is not evidence of agreement.
- [[mimetic-desire|Most wants are borrowed from other people]]. Mimetic desire is wanting something because someone else wants it or has it, which makes a desire feel personal while being borrowed.
- [[reputation-momentum|Reputation holds while momentum looks like approval]]. A public figure can survive a serious accusation while people still watch and buy, because momentum reads as the crowd still approving.

## Telling the truth

- [[the-cassandra-effect|Accurate warnings are dismissed when early or from a low-status source]]. Valid warnings are ignored when the threat is not yet visible or the warner lacks standing, and the more dire the warning, the less it is believed.
- [[the-messenger-paradox|Groups that punish messengers stop hearing bad news]]. People who deliver accurate bad news get punished, which removes the early warning an organisation needs and leads to crises it never saw coming.
- [[toxic-compassion|Compassion that avoids truth does harm]]. Putting short-term emotional comfort ahead of reality and long-term outcomes optimises for looking good rather than doing good.
- [[kindness-vs-choice|Is kindness you are forced into still kindness?]]. A reflection on whether a good act is virtuous when your nature compels it rather than your choice, left without an answer.
- [[milgram-questions|A question that punishes honest answers cannot be answered honestly]]. A Milgram question has a social penalty for an unflattering answer that exceeds the reward for the truth, so its answers cannot be trusted.

## Judging ideas and sources

- [[rules-before-exceptions|Intuition is earned by following the rules first]]. Rules supply the repetitions that make intuition possible, and breaking them before learning them is usually just not playing the game.
- [[trust-people-who-change-their-minds|Trust thinkers who change their minds and admit mistakes]]. Four questions separate rational truth-seekers from ideological charlatans among the creators you follow.
- [[judge-content-by-how-you-feel-after|Judge content by how you feel after consuming it]]. Compelling content is not necessarily good for you, and the way to tell is to check how you feel once it ends.
- [[cynicism-vs-hope|Cynicism protects against disappointment and excuses inaction]]. Online cynicism is a guarded response that spares you the risk of trying, and choosing hope without naivety is the better way to live.
- [[proximate-vs-ultimate|Behaviour has a proximate mechanism and an ultimate reason]]. Understanding human behaviour needs both how it works in the moment and why evolution shaped it that way, and the two do not conflict.
- [[astrologers-no-better-than-chance|Experienced astrologers did no better than chance]]. In a study of 152 astrologers matching people to natal charts, the group scored 20 percent, the same as random guessing.
- [[the-eliza-effect|People read understanding into simple pattern-matching]]. The Eliza effect is reading far more understanding into computer responses than exists, and knowing about it does not make you immune.

## Boundaries

- [[apology-is-not-accountability|An apology is not accountability and forgiveness is not access]]. Regret and good words do not show a pattern has changed, so trust follows behaviour and forgiveness need not restore access.
- [[boundary-scripts|Boundary scripts]]. Four lists for dealing with difficult people. Traits to avoid, replies to victim mentality, ego-checking phrases and manipulation tactics.
